## Draft guide: What is a good Core Web Vitals score in 2026?

*Tier 2, guide #1 per seo-content-plan.md priority order (first one drafted — no

**Suggested title tag:** What Is a Good Core Web Vitals Score in 2026? (LCP, INP, CLS Thresholds) | Site Mechanic
**Suggested meta description:** The real 2026 Core Web Vitals thresholds for LCP, INP, and CLS — what "good" actually means, why your score moves between runs, and how to check yours free in 30 seconds.

---

Google's Core Web Vitals haven't changed in *definition* since Interaction to Next
Paint (INP) replaced First Input Delay in March 2024, but the bar for "good" is
higher than most teams think, and the metric people obsess over — Lighthouse's
performance score — isn't the one Google actually uses to rank you. Here's what the
three vitals mean in 2026, what thresholds actually matter, and why two runs of the
same page can disagree.

### The three metrics, and the thresholds that matter

**Largest Contentful Paint (LCP)** measures how long it takes the biggest visible
element — usually a hero image, a heading, or a video poster — to render. Google's
threshold: **2.5 seconds or faster is "good,"** 2.5–4s is "needs improvement," and
anything past 4s is "poor." LCP is scored at the 75th percentile of real visits, not
your best run in DevTools — a page that loads in 1.8s on your gigabit connection can
still fail LCP if your median mobile visitor is on a throttled 4G connection with a
cold cache.

**Interaction to Next Paint (INP)** measures responsiveness — the delay between a
user's click, tap, or keypress and the next time the browser paints a visual
response. Threshold: **200ms or under is good,** 200–500ms is "needs improvement,"
above 500ms is "poor." INP replaced First Input Delay because FID only measured the
*first* interaction; INP scores the worst (or near-worst) interaction across the
whole session, which is why sites with heavy JavaScript that loads fine but then
janks on every click score badly here even when FID would have looked fine.

**Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS)** measures visual stability — how much visible
content jumps around unexpectedly as the page loads. Threshold: **0.1 or under is
good,** 0.1–0.25 is "needs improvement," above 0.25 is "poor." CLS is usually the
cheapest of the three to fix and the most embarrassing to leave broken: it's almost
always an image or ad slot with no reserved `width`/`height`, or a web font swapping
in and reflowing text a beat after first paint.

### "Good" means all three, at the 75th percentile

A page doesn't pass Core Web Vitals by averaging well — it needs LCP, INP, *and*
CLS to individually hit "good" at the 75th percentile of real-user visits over a
rolling 28-day window, per device type (mobile and desktop are scored separately).
Google's own data puts roughly a third of sites passing all three simultaneously as
of 2026 — up from prior years, but still meaning most sites fail on at least one
metric even when the other two are strong. If your homepage nails LCP and CLS but
INP sits at 340ms because of a heavy JS bundle, the page still fails the overall
assessment.

### Field data vs. lab data — why your score keeps changing

This is the single biggest source of confusion. Lighthouse (and any tool built on
it, including the "Performance" tab in Chrome DevTools) runs a **lab test** — one
simulated load, on a simulated network throttle, on whatever machine is running the
test. Google's actual ranking signal comes from **field data**: real Chrome users,
aggregated in the Chrome UX Report (CrUX), over a rolling 28-day window.

A few consequences that trip people up:

- **A single Lighthouse run isn't your Core Web Vitals score.** It's an estimate of
  what a real visit *might* look like under specific throttling assumptions. Two
  runs 30 seconds apart can differ by 10-15 points just from CPU contention on
  whatever's running the test, ad-blocker state, or a CDN edge cache miss.
- **CrUX only reports on sites with enough traffic.** Low-traffic pages (and most
  local-business or B2B sites) simply don't have a CrUX-reported score — Search
  Console will show "not enough data," and lab tools are the only signal you get.
- **Field scores lag deploys by weeks.** Ship a fix today and Search Console's
  Core Web Vitals report won't reflect it for up to 28 days, since it's a rolling
  window of real visits.

If you're chasing a specific Lighthouse number in CI, you're optimizing a proxy —
useful for catching regressions before they ship, but not the same thing Google
scores you on.

### The fixes that actually move the needle

In rough order of impact for most sites:

1. **LCP** — preload the LCP image or font (`<link rel="preload">`), avoid
   render-blocking CSS/JS above the fold, and make sure your server's
   time-to-first-byte isn't the bottleneck (a slow TTFB caps everything downstream
   of it, no matter how optimized the rest of the page is).
2. **INP** — break up long JavaScript tasks (anything over 50ms blocks the main
   thread), defer non-critical third-party scripts, and audit what's running on
   every keystroke in search boxes or filters — that's the most common INP killer
   we see.
3. **CLS** — set explicit `width`/`height` (or `aspect-ratio`) on every image and
   embed, reserve space for ads/embeds before they load, and use `font-display:
   swap` carefully — swap avoids invisible text but can still shift layout if the
   fallback and web font have different metrics; `size-adjust` in `@font-face` can
   close that gap.
