### 1. Ask HN: Do people care about image optimisation anymore?
https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49595998 — posted today (Sep 7), 3 points, no replies yet.

**Summary:** OP (CM30) notes that the old discipline — JPEGs for photos, PNGs for icons/illustrations,
SVGs for vectors, images sized for their container — seems to have quietly died. Sites now dump
multi-megabyte PNG photos and full-res GIFs onto pages and shrug, apparently assuming everyone's
on fast fiber. They're asking whether anyone still cares.

**Drafted reply (from Peter's account):**
> People still care, but it's stopped being a default habit — it's become something you only
> catch if you're specifically looking for it. In practice the "upload the PNG straight off the
> camera roll" pattern is still extremely common; CMS uploads and screenshot-paste flows make it
> the path of least resistance, and most frameworks don't warn you.
>
> The place this actually bites is LCP — for most pages the largest contentful element is a
> hero image or a big photo in the body, and an unoptimized image is often the single biggest
> lever on that number. I run a lot of site scans (built sitemechanic.io, a free performance/SEO/accessibility/security
> checker) and oversized, wrong-format images are consistently the top flagged issue on the
> performance side — more often than JS bloat or server response time.
>
> If you want a quick gut-check on a specific image, Squoosh (squoosh.app) is still the fastest
> way to see the JPEG/WebP/AVIF size tradeoff side by side before you commit to a format.

*Mentioning sitemechanic.io here is earned — the question is literally "does anyone check this
anymore," and "here's what an automated scan actually finds most often" answers it with data,
not a pitch. Squoosh is named because it's genuinely the better tool for the specific sub-task

### 2. Website accessibility certification: facade, charade, or just virtue signaling?
https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49520821 — posted Sep 1, article at replyresearch.com, 2 points, no comments yet.

**Summary:** Article argues that a lot of "accessibility certified" badges and overlay-widget
products give site owners a false sense of legal/compliance safety without fixing (or even
detecting) the underlying WCAG failures — sometimes making things worse for screen reader users.

**Drafted reply (from Peter's account):**
> This matches what I've seen from the tooling side. There's a real gap between "an automated
> scan came back clean" and "this site is actually usable with a screen reader or keyboard-only" —
> automated checks (axe-core, Lighthouse, and my own accessibility analyzer at sitemechanic.io
> all included) can only catch the subset of WCAG 2.1 failures that are structurally detectable:
> missing alt text, contrast ratios, missing form labels, heading order, that kind of thing. They
> can't tell you if your custom dropdown actually works with a screen reader, or if your focus
> order makes sense. Overlay widgets are worse than that — some of them actively interfere with
> assistive tech instead of fixing the markup.
>
> The honest framing is: automated scan = fast way to find the obvious stuff and triage, not a
> compliance certificate. Anyone claiming otherwise is selling the badge, not the accessibility.
> For anything customer-facing or ADA-exposed, pair the automated pass with actual manual testing
> (WAVE's manual checklist or a real screen reader pass) before you tell legal you're "compliant."

*Mention is honest and self-deprecating about the category's limits — it argues against
overclaiming, which is the opposite of a pitch, and that's why it fits here.*

### Draft body (~1,230 words)

Every year someone posts a "Core Web Vitals just changed!" article with a scary new threshold
number, and every year most of it turns out to be unsourced. So before anything else: here are
the actual current thresholds, straight from Google's own web.dev documentation, current as of
this writing.

**The three metrics, and what "good" means:**

| Metric | Good | Needs improvement | Poor |
|---|---|---|---|
| LCP (Largest Contentful Paint) | ≤ 2.5s | 2.5s – 4.0s | > 4.0s |
| INP (Interaction to Next Paint) | ≤ 200ms | 200ms – 500ms | > 500ms |
| CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift) | ≤ 0.1 | 0.1 – 0.25 | > 0.25 |

If you've seen claims that the "good" LCP threshold dropped to 2.0 seconds, or that Google added
a new metric called "Engagement Reliability" in 2026 — we went looking for a primary source on
both and couldn't find one. Google's own vitals documentation, last updated in the tools we
checked, still lists the numbers above. A lot of SEO content this year is repeating those claims
without a citation. Don't fail a launch checklist over a threshold nobody can point to in
Google's docs. If Google actually changes these numbers, it'll show up on web.dev and in Search
Console guidance first — check there, not on a blog.

**What actually changed, and matters:** INP replaced FID as the official interactivity metric
back in March 2024, and that's real and settled — if you're still optimizing for FID, you're
optimizing for a retired metric. INP is stricter and broader: FID only measured the delay before
the *first* interaction started processing; INP measures the full cost of processing *every*
interaction during the page's lifetime, including the work after the input handler starts
(rendering, layout, paint) — and reports the worst-case at roughly the 98th percentile of
interactions on that page load. A page can have great FID and mediocre INP if it does a lot of
work in response to clicks later in the session (opening modals, filtering a list, submitting a
form). If you haven't checked your INP specifically since 2024, it's worth doing now — it's a
different metric with different failure modes, not a rename of FID.

**"75th percentile" is doing more work than people realize.** Google doesn't grade your site on
its best load, or even its average load — it takes the 75th percentile of real-user page loads,
measured separately for mobile and desktop, over a rolling 28-day window (this is the Chrome
User Experience Report, CrUX). That has two practical consequences that trip people up:

1. **You can't "test" your way to a CWV pass.** A single Lighthouse run in dev tools, on your
   fast office wifi, tells you almost nothing about your 75th-percentile real-user experience. It's
   a lab test. Your actual score depends on your real visitors' real devices and real networks —
   which for most sites skews toward older Android phones on patchy 4G, not the machine you're
   developing on. If you want a number that's actually predictive, you need field data (CrUX,
   Search Console's Core Web Vitals report, or a real-user-monitoring script), not just repeated
   lab runs.

2. **Your worst 25% counts against you, disproportionately.** If your homepage is fast but your
   product pages have a slow LCP because of unoptimized images (still the single most common LCP
   killer — see the note above), the aggregate 75th-percentile figure for that URL group will
   reflect the slow pages, not just the fast one you keep checking. Fix your worst-performing
   templates, not your fastest ones.

**Where the failures actually cluster, in order of how often we see them in scans:**

- **LCP, from images.** An unoptimized hero image or product photo — wrong format (PNG where
  WebP/AVIF would be smaller), not sized for its container, not preloaded, or lazy-loaded when it
  shouldn't be (never lazy-load your LCP element — that delays it further). This is the most
  common failure by a wide margin.
- **LCP, from render-blocking resources.** Synchronous third-party scripts and unoptimized web
  fonts (see the render-blocking-fonts guide, next in this series) sitting in `<head>` and
  delaying first paint of everything, including the LCP element.
- **CLS, from late-loading content shoving things around.** Ads, embeds, and web fonts that swap
  in a different-sized fallback font are the usual suspects. Reserve space (explicit width/height
  on images and embeds, `font-display: optional` or matched fallback metrics for fonts) so the
  layout doesn't jump after paint.
- **INP, from long tasks on interaction.** Heavy client-side JS running synchronously in response
  to a click — building a whole component tree, running an unthrottled search filter, blocking
  the main thread on a large JSON parse. Break the work up, defer what isn't needed for the next
  paint, and move genuinely heavy work off the main thread.

**A good target, honestly:** aim to pass all three metrics — that's the bar Google actually
uses in Search Console's Core Web Vitals report and, since 2021, as a (modest, one-of-many-hundred)
ranking signal. Passing "all three" means your 75th-percentile mobile and desktop numbers both
land in the "good" column for LCP, INP, and CLS simultaneously — CrUX data currently puts a bit
over half of tracked sites in that category, so passing isn't table stakes, it's a real
differentiator, but it's also achievable without a full rewrite for most sites: image
optimization and font-loading fixes alone close most of the gap for a typical content or
marketing site.

**Checking your own numbers:** Search Console's Core Web Vitals report gives you real CrUX field
data by URL group, which is the ground truth — start there if you have access. If you don't (or
you're checking a site you don't have Search Console on, like a client site or a competitor), a
lab-based scan will still catch the specific issues — oversized images, render-blocking fonts,
layout-shifting embeds — even though it can't give you the real 75th-percentile field number.

**A five-minute checklist before you go digging further:**

1. Open your site's Search Console → Core Web Vitals report (or run a scan if you don't have
   Search Console access) and note which of the three metrics is actually failing — don't
   guess. Fixing CLS when your real problem is LCP wastes a sprint.
2. If LCP is the failure: find the actual LCP element (Chrome DevTools Performance panel labels
   it) and check whether it's an image. If so, check its format, whether it's sized for its
   render dimensions, and whether it's preloaded rather than lazy-loaded.
3. If CLS is the failure: check for images and embeds without explicit width/height, and web
   fonts without a matched fallback (a swapped-in fallback font that's a different width causes
   reflow the instant the real font loads).
4. If INP is the failure: profile what happens on the interaction that's slow — most of the time
   it's one specific click handler doing too much synchronous work, not a general "the whole
   site is slow" problem.
5. Re-test after each fix with field data if you can get it, not just a single lab run — a lab
   score improving doesn't guarantee your 75th-percentile real-user number moved.

None of this requires a rewrite. For most sites, steps 2 and 3 alone — image format/sizing and
font-loading fixes — close most of the gap between "needs improvement" and "good."
