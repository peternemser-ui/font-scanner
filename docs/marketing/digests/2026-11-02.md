### What is a good Core Web Vitals score in 2026?

If you've pasted your URL into PageSpeed Insights and gotten a score that doesn't
match what your Lighthouse run in Chrome DevTools says, you're not imagining it —
and you're not doing anything wrong. These two numbers are allowed to disagree,
because they're measuring different things. This guide covers what "good" actually
means for Core Web Vitals in 2026, where the thresholds come from, and why the
gap between lab and field data trips up more developers than the metrics themselves.

#### The three metrics, and their thresholds

Core Web Vitals is three metrics, not one score:

- **LCP (Largest Contentful Paint)** — how long until the largest visible element
  (usually a hero image, a heading, or a block of text) has rendered.
  - Good: ≤ 2.5s
  - Needs improvement: 2.5s–4s
  - Poor: > 4s

- **INP (Interaction to Next Paint)** — how long the page takes to visibly respond
  after a user clicks, taps, or types. INP replaced FID (First Input Delay) as an
  official Core Web Vital in March 2024, and it's the one most teams haven't
  finished adapting to — FID only measured the delay before the first interaction
  started processing; INP measures the full round trip for *every* interaction
  across the page's lifetime, which is a much harder bar to clear on JS-heavy pages.
  - Good: ≤ 200ms
  - Needs improvement: 200ms–500ms
  - Poor: > 500ms

- **CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift)** — how much visible content jumps around
  unexpectedly as the page loads (late-loading ads, web fonts swapping in, images
  without reserved dimensions).
  - Good: ≤ 0.1
  - Needs improvement: 0.1–0.25
  - Poor: > 0.25

A page "passes" Core Web Vitals when **at least 75% of real visits** hit the "good"
threshold on all three metrics simultaneously — not on average, and not in a single
test run.

#### Why your Lighthouse score and your Core Web Vitals status disagree

This is the part that confuses the most people, and it's not a bug: Lighthouse and
Core Web Vitals use different data sources entirely.

- **Lighthouse is lab data.** It runs a single simulated page load, on simulated
  network/CPU conditions, in a controlled environment, right now. It's useful for
  debugging a page you're actively working on.
- **Core Web Vitals (the ones that affect how Google treats your page) are field
  data,** sourced from the Chrome User Experience Report (CrUX) — real Chrome users,
  real devices, real networks, aggregated over the trailing 28 days.

A page can score 100 in Lighthouse and still fail its real-world Core Web Vitals,
because Lighthouse never saw the 3G connection, the five-year-old Android phone, or
the ad-blocker-less visitor your actual field data includes. The reverse happens
too — a page with a mediocre Lighthouse score can still pass CWV in the field if its
real audience mostly has fast connections and modern hardware.

One more wrinkle: if your site doesn't get enough Chrome traffic, CrUX has no field
data for it at all, and tools that rely purely on CrUX will show nothing. That's
one reason it's worth running both a lab (Lighthouse-based) check *and* checking for
field data separately — they tell you different, complementary things.

#### Mobile and desktop are graded separately

CWV data is segmented by mobile vs. desktop, and most sites' mobile numbers are
visibly worse — mobile devices and networks are slower, and Google's "good"
thresholds are the same for both, so mobile is the harder bar. If you're only
checking desktop Lighthouse scores, you're grading on the easy version.

#### What actually moves these numbers

In rough order of typical impact:

1. **LCP** — compress and properly size your hero image/largest element, serve it in
   a modern format (AVIF/WebP), and make sure it isn't blocked behind render-blocking
   CSS or late-discovered fonts. Preload it if it's not discoverable from the initial
   HTML.
2. **INP** — break up long JavaScript tasks (anything over ~50ms blocking the main
   thread), defer non-critical scripts, and avoid attaching expensive work to click/
   input handlers. Third-party scripts (analytics, chat widgets, ad tags) are the
   most common offender here, not your own code.
3. **CLS** — reserve space for images and embeds with explicit width/height or
   `aspect-ratio`, avoid injecting content above existing content after load, and
   watch for layout shift caused by web fonts swapping in (`font-display: optional`
   or preloading your font files avoids most of this).

#### What if CrUX has no data for your site?

Small sites, new sites, and internal tools often don't get enough Chrome traffic to
show up in CrUX at all — Google only publishes a CWV status once a URL or origin
clears a minimum traffic threshold. If PageSpeed Insights shows "Field Data: Not
Available" for your page, that's not a pass or a fail, it's a "we don't know yet."
In that situation:

- Fall back to **origin-level** CrUX data (your whole domain) instead of the
  specific URL, if it's available — it's a reasonable proxy for how similar pages
  on your site will perform.
- Treat your Lighthouse/lab score as the best available signal, but remember it's
  a single run under simulated conditions — run it a few times and look at the
  spread, not just one number, since lab runs do vary.
- Prioritize fixing the structural issues (unsized images, long main-thread tasks,
  render-blocking fonts) anyway. They cause poor CWV *and* a poor Lighthouse score,
  so you don't need field data to know they're worth fixing.

#### Common mistakes that skew the numbers

- **Testing on your dev machine.** A fast laptop on fiber will out-perform the real
  mix of devices visiting your site, mobile especially. Throttle your test, or
  trust the field data over your own lived experience of the page.
- **Chasing the Lighthouse score to 100.** Past a certain point you're optimizing a
  simulated single page load instead of the three metrics that actually affect how
  Google treats the page. Diminishing returns set in fast.
- **Fixing LCP by preloading everything.** Preload is a scarce resource — preload
  your actual LCP element (usually one image or one block of text) and its
  dependencies, not every asset on the page. Preloading too much can delay the one
  thing that matters.
- **Ignoring INP because FID used to pass.** If your site passed on FID before
  March 2024, that tells you nothing about INP. INP is measured across every
  interaction on the page, not just the first one, so pages with a lot of
  client-side interactivity (filters, modals, infinite scroll) often regress here
  even though nothing about the page's "first load" changed.

#### A minimal checklist

1. Check field data first (PageSpeed Insights or Search Console's Core Web Vitals
   report) — that's what actually counts.
2. If there's no field data, use lab data as your best guess, with the caveats
   above.
3. Fix LCP: size and compress the largest element, avoid blocking it behind fonts
   or render-blocking CSS.
4. Fix INP: find and break up long JavaScript tasks, especially from third-party
   scripts.
5. Fix CLS: reserve space for anything that loads asynchronously, including fonts.
6. Re-test mobile specifically — don't assume desktop numbers generalize.
