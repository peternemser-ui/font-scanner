If you've ever gotten a 98 in Lighthouse and then watched Search Console tell you your
site "Needs Improvement," you're not imagining things — you're looking at two
different measurement systems with two different definitions of "good." This guide
covers what Google actually checks in 2026, the real thresholds, and why the numbers
disagree.

### The three metrics, and the current thresholds

Core Web Vitals has been three metrics since March 2024, when Interaction to Next
Paint (INP) replaced First Input Delay (FID). The current "good" thresholds, measured
at the 75th percentile of page loads:

- **LCP (Largest Contentful Paint)** — time until the largest visible element
  (usually a hero image, a headline, or a background image) finishes rendering.
  Good: **≤ 2.5s**. Needs improvement: 2.5s–4s. Poor: > 4s.
- **INP (Interaction to Next Paint)** — the latency of the *slowest* interaction on
  the page (click, tap, keypress) across the entire visit, not just the first one.
  Good: **≤ 200ms**. Needs improvement: 200ms–500ms. Poor: > 500ms.
- **CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift)** — a unitless score summing up unexpected layout
  movement during the page's lifetime. Good: **≤ 0.1**. Needs improvement: 0.1–0.25.
  Poor: > 0.25.

To "pass" Core Web Vitals as a page or origin, all three need to be in the "good"
bucket at the 75th percentile — not on average. That 75th-percentile detail matters:
a site that's fast for most visitors but slow for one in four (say, everyone on a
throttled connection, or everyone hitting a cold cache) can still fail.

### Why your Lighthouse score and Search Console disagree

This is the single most common source of confusion, and it's not a bug in either
tool — they're measuring different things:

- **Lighthouse / PageSpeed Insights "lab" score** is a simulated run, once, from a
  fixed network/CPU profile, on whatever machine ran the test. It's useful for
  debugging *why* something is slow, because you get a full waterfall and
  opportunities list. It is not what Google uses for ranking.
- **Search Console's Core Web Vitals report** is real-user field data (from the
  Chrome User Experience Report — CrUX), aggregated over the *previous 28 days*
  across real visitors on real devices and real networks. This is what actually
  feeds into the page experience ranking signal.

A page can lab-test at LCP 1.8s on a fast dev machine and field-report LCP 3.9s
because half your real traffic is on mid-range Android phones on 4G. The lab score
tells you what's technically possible; the field score tells you what's actually
happening. If you only ever check Lighthouse, you're flying blind on the number that
matters for search.

One more wrinkle: CrUX field data needs a minimum sample size to report at all. Low-
traffic pages (and most pages on most sites) won't show up individually in the report
— only the origin-level aggregate will. If Search Console shows "no data" for a URL,
that doesn't mean it's fine, it means Google doesn't have enough real visits to say
either way.

### The metrics developers get wrong most often

**LCP** — the biggest miss is treating LCP as a "make things load faster" problem
when it's usually a "make the browser realize what the LCP element is, sooner"
problem. If your hero image is set via CSS `background-image` instead of an `<img>`
tag, or if it's injected by client-side JS after hydration, the browser can't
discover and prioritize it early. `fetchpriority="high"` and `<link rel="preload">`
on the actual LCP resource, discoverable in the initial HTML, fixes more LCP problems
than shrinking image file size does.

**INP** — this one punishes long JavaScript tasks anywhere on the page, not just on
first load. A single client-side router with a heavy re-render on route change, or a
poorly debounced input handler, will tank INP on a page that scored perfectly on
first paint. Break up long tasks with `scheduler.yield()` (or `setTimeout(fn, 0)` as
a fallback) so the main thread can respond to input between chunks of work.

**CLS** — almost always one of three causes: images or ads without explicit
`width`/`height` (or `aspect-ratio`), web fonts swapping in with different metrics
than the fallback, or content injected above existing content (cookie banners,
"you might also like" widgets) after initial layout. `font-display: optional` or
matched fallback-font metrics fix the font case; reserving space up front fixes the
other two.

### What actually moves the field-data number

Because CrUX is a rolling 28-day window of real users, a fix you ship today won't
show up in Search Console for weeks, and it takes the full window to fully reflect a
change. That lag is normal — it's not a sign your fix didn't work. The fastest way to
verify a fix worked is lab data (re-run Lighthouse, check the specific metric moved
in the direction you expect) followed by patience on the field number.
