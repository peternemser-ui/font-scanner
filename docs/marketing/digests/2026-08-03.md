## Draft guide: What is a good Core Web Vitals score in 2026?

**Suggested title tag:** What Is a Good Core Web Vitals Score in 2026? (LCP, INP, CLS Thresholds)
**Suggested meta description:** The actual 2026 pass/fail thresholds for LCP, INP, and CLS, why your lab score and field score disagree, and how to check where your site really stands — free, no signup.

---

Google's Core Web Vitals thresholds haven't moved much since INP replaced FID in 2024, but the goalposts around them keep getting relitigated in blog posts that don't cite where the numbers come from. Here's what's actually true in 2026, and — more usefully — what the numbers mean when your own site doesn't hit them.

### The three metrics and their thresholds

**Largest Contentful Paint (LCP)** measures how long it takes the biggest visible element on the page — usually a hero image, a heading block, or a banner — to fully render. Google's thresholds:

- Good: 2.5 seconds or less
- Needs improvement: 2.5–4 seconds
- Poor: over 4 seconds

**Interaction to Next Paint (INP)** measures responsiveness — the time from a user's click, tap, or keypress to the next frame the browser paints in response. It replaced First Input Delay (FID) because FID only measured the *first* interaction's delay, not whether a site stayed responsive throughout a session. Thresholds:

- Good: 200ms or less
- Needs improvement: 200–500ms
- Poor: over 500ms

**Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS)** measures visual stability — how much visible content jumps around unexpectedly as a page loads (images without dimensions, web fonts swapping in, ads injecting late). Thresholds:

- Good: 0.1 or less
- Needs improvement: 0.1–0.25
- Poor: over 0.25

To "pass" Core Web Vitals as a ranking signal, Google's own documentation is specific: at least 75% of page visits (weighted across mobile and desktop) need to hit the "Good" threshold for **all three** metrics. Passing two out of three doesn't count.

### Why your Lighthouse score and your Search Console numbers disagree

This is the single most common source of confusion, and it's worth being precise about it because the two numbers are measuring genuinely different things.

**Lighthouse and PageSpeed Insights report lab data.** It's a single simulated run, on a fixed (throttled) connection and device profile, from wherever Google's testing infrastructure happens to be that day. It's reproducible and great for debugging a specific change, but it is not what your users experience.

**Search Console and the Chrome UX Report (CrUX) report field data.** This is real telemetry from actual Chrome users who visited your site with Chrome's usage-statistics reporting enabled, aggregated over a rolling 28-day window. It's what Google actually uses for the ranking signal.

A page can score 100 in Lighthouse and still fail Core Web Vitals in the field, because:

1. Your test device (usually a fast desktop or a mid-tier throttle profile) doesn't match your real audience's phones and connections.
2. A single lab run can't capture variance — a slow third-party script that only sometimes blocks the main thread won't always show up.
3. Field data lags by design — you're always looking at the last 28 days, not today. Ship a fix and you won't see it reflected in Search Console for weeks.

None of this means Lighthouse is useless — it's the right tool for fast iteration while you're actively fixing something. It just isn't the tool that tells you whether you're passing.

### The fixes that actually move the needle, in order of typical impact

- **LCP:** compress and correctly size the LCP image, serve modern formats (WebP/AVIF), add `fetchpriority="high"` to the LCP element and *never* lazy-load it, and fix slow server response times — no front-end optimization compensates for a server that takes 2 seconds to respond before the browser can even start rendering.
- **INP:** break up long JavaScript tasks, defer non-critical third-party scripts (chat widgets and analytics are frequent offenders), and avoid heavy work in event handlers.
- **CLS:** set explicit `width`/`height` (or `aspect-ratio`) on every image and embed, reserve space for ads and dynamically injected content, and use `font-display: swap` or `optional` deliberately — an unstyled-to-styled font swap is one of the most common causes of layout shift that developers don't think to check.

### Checking where you actually stand

If you want field data, Search Console's Core Web Vitals report is the source of truth, but it requires site verification and a 28-day accumulation window before you get a meaningful sample. If you want a same-day lab check across LCP, INP, and CLS — plus a look at whether font loading or render-blocking resources are dragging your numbers down — [run a free scan with Site Mechanic's Core Web Vitals analyzer](https://sitemechanic.io/core-web-vitals.html). No signup, results in about 30 seconds, and it flags the specific element or resource responsible for each metric rather than just the score.
