### 4. Nordwand Mono — a monospace font for coders (Google Fonts self-hosting subthread)
**Link:** https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50163381 (subthread under https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50141581, posted 2026-10-20)
**Summary:** Show HN for a new coding font; a side thread debates whether to list it on Google Fonts, with several commenters noting self-hosting now beats loading from Google's CDN on both performance and privacy grounds.

**Drafted reply:**
> Self-hosting is the right call here, and it's a bigger win than people expect for a monospace/coding font specifically — these tend to carry large glyph sets (box-drawing characters, ligatures, wide Unicode coverage), so the WOFF2 is often heavier than a typical text font, and Google's CDN serves the full charset rather than subsetting to what a page actually uses. Self-hosting plus `font-display: swap` plus subsetting to just the ranges you need can cut that payload a lot, and it also removes the extra DNS/TLS handshake to fonts.gstatic.com entirely. Fontsource is the easiest path if you still want npm-package convenience without the CDN. (I added a font-loading check to my own site scanner for exactly this reason — render-blocking, non-subsetted web fonts are one of the most common and most fixable issues I see in real audits.)

## Draft guide: What is a good Core Web Vitals score in 2026?

**Suggested title tag:** `Good Core Web Vitals Score in 2026: LCP, INP & CLS Thresholds | Site Mechanic`
**Suggested meta description:** `The real LCP, INP, and CLS thresholds for 2026 — and why your Lighthouse 99 isn't the same thing as passing Core Web Vitals. Free field-vs-lab checker included.`

---

If you've shipped a page that scores 95+ in Lighthouse and still isn't "passing" Core
Web Vitals in Search Console, you've run into the most common point of confusion in
performance work: a lab score and a Core Web Vitals assessment are not the same
measurement, and only one of them is what Google actually uses.

### The three metrics, and the thresholds that haven't changed

Despite a wave of blog posts claiming a "2026 update" to Core Web Vitals, there's no
official change to the metrics or the thresholds. The three vitals, and what counts as
"good" at the 75th percentile, are:

- **LCP (Largest Contentful Paint)** — time until the largest visible element (usually
  a hero image or heading) renders. Good: **≤ 2.5s**. Poor: **> 4.0s**.
- **INP (Interaction to Next Paint)** — the responsiveness of the slowest (or
  near-slowest) interaction during the page's lifetime. Good: **≤ 200ms**. Poor:
  **> 500ms**. INP replaced FID in March 2024, and Search Console stopped tracking FID
  at that point — if you're still optimizing for FID, you're optimizing for a retired
  metric.
- **CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift)** — how much visible content jumps around during
  load and interaction. Good: **≤ 0.1**. Poor: **> 0.25**.

A page "passes" Core Web Vitals when **75% of real visits** meet the "good" threshold
for all three. Not the average visit — the 75th percentile, which means your slower
visitors (older devices, worse connections) count, and a handful of fast loads from
your own desk don't rescue a bad distribution.

### Why your Lighthouse 99 and your Core Web Vitals status can disagree

This is the part most guides skip, and it's the actual source of confusion:

**Lighthouse gives you lab data.** It's one simulated run, on Google's test
infrastructure, with fixed CPU and network throttling meant to approximate a mid-range
phone. It's deterministic-ish, repeatable, and great for debugging *because* it holds
conditions constant. But it is not a measurement of what your real visitors
experienced.

**The Core Web Vitals report in Search Console (and the CrUX box in PageSpeed
Insights) is field data.** It's aggregated from the Chrome User Experience Report —
real Chrome users, on their own devices and networks, over a trailing **28-day
window**. This is what Google actually uses for the page-experience signal, and it's
why:

- A brand-new page can score 99 in Lighthouse and show *no* Core Web Vitals data at
  all in Search Console — it hasn't had enough real traffic yet for CrUX to report on
  it.
- Two pages with identical code can have different CWV status if one gets a lot of
  traffic from a market with worse average connections.
- Your score can look worse a month *after* a genuine fix ships, simply because the
  28-day window is still partly covering the old, slower version.

If you only ever check Lighthouse, you're checking the thing that's useful for
debugging but isn't the thing being graded. If you only check Search Console, you
won't catch a regression for up to 28 days. You need both, read correctly, not one
instead of the other.

### What actually moves each metric, briefly

- **LCP:** the single biggest lever is almost always the hero image or font for your
  largest above-the-fold element. Serve it at the right size (don't make the browser
  downscale a huge image), skip lazy-loading on it specifically, and consider
  `fetchpriority="high"` plus a `<link rel="preload">` for it. Server response time
  (TTFB) is the second lever — a slow origin or a CDN edge that isn't actually caching
  what you think it is will cap your LCP no matter what you do client-side.
- **INP:** look for long JavaScript tasks that run on input — a single heavy event
  handler, a synchronous layout read inside a click handler, or a huge hydration pass
  on page load that blocks the main thread right when someone tries to interact.
  Breaking up long tasks (`requestIdleCallback`, yielding with `scheduler.yield()`
  where available, or just doing less work synchronously) is the usual fix.
- **CLS:** almost always late-arriving content without reserved space — images and
  iframes without explicit `width`/`height` (or `aspect-ratio`), web fonts that swap in
  at a different metric than the fallback (fixable with `size-adjust` and the other
  font-metric-override descriptors), and ads or embeds injected after initial render
  without a placeholder.

### The honest caveat

Core Web Vitals genuinely is one of many ranking signals, and Google has said
repeatedly that relevance and content quality outweigh it. Chasing a perfect score on a
page nobody wants to read is a waste of a sprint. But it's also not nothing: it's one
of the few ranking factors you have full engineering control over, and a page that's
unpleasant to use tends to convert and retain worse regardless of what it does for
search — so it's worth getting right for the visitor, not just for Google.
