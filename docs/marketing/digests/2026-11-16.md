### What Is a Good Core Web Vitals Score in 2026?

If you've pasted a URL into PageSpeed Insights and gotten a wall of red and orange bars, you've probably asked the obvious question: what number am I actually supposed to be hitting? The honest answer is simpler than most SEO blog posts make it sound, and it hasn't changed much since INP replaced FID back in March 2024 — despite some unverified claims floating around this year that Google quietly tightened the LCP threshold. It didn't, as far as any primary Google source confirms. Here's what's actually true, how it's measured, and where real sites typically go wrong.

#### The three metrics and their thresholds

Google's Core Web Vitals are three numbers, each with a "good," "needs improvement," and "poor" band, measured at the **75th percentile** of real visits to a page — not a single lab test, and not an average.

- **LCP (Largest Contentful Paint)** — how long until the largest visible element (usually a hero image, a block of text, or a video poster) finishes rendering.
  - Good: ≤ 2.5s
  - Needs improvement: 2.5s–4.0s
  - Poor: > 4.0s

- **INP (Interaction to Next Paint)** — how long the page takes to visually respond after a user clicks, taps, or types, measured across all interactions during the page's lifetime (not just the first one — that was FID's job, and it's retired).
  - Good: ≤ 200ms
  - Needs improvement: 200ms–500ms
  - Poor: > 500ms

- **CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift)** — how much visible content jumps around unexpectedly as the page loads (images without dimensions, late-loading ads, web fonts swapping in at a different size).
  - Good: ≤ 0.1
  - Needs improvement: 0.1–0.25
  - Poor: > 0.25

That "75th percentile" detail matters more than most guides mention. It means a page doesn't pass because your one test run in Chrome DevTools looked fast — it passes when three out of four real visitors, across whatever devices and connections they actually have, experience it as fast. A site that's instant on your office fiber connection and your developer laptop can still fail CWV outright if a meaningful chunk of its real traffic is on mid-range Android phones over spotty 4G. This is also why two people can run the "same" audit tool on the same URL minutes apart and get different numbers: lab tools (Lighthouse, PageSpeed Insights' lab section) simulate one device/network profile once, while the Core Web Vitals that actually affect your Search Console report and your ranking are field data aggregated over 28 days of real users (via the Chrome UX Report, CrUX). If you want a number that tracks what Google is actually judging, you need field data — a one-off lab test is a proxy for debugging, not a scorecard.

#### Where the data actually comes from

There are two sources, and conflating them is the single most common mistake:

1. **Field data (CrUX)** — real Chrome users who've opted into usage reporting, aggregated by Google over a rolling 28-day window. This is what Search Console's Core Web Vitals report shows you, and it's the thing search ranking signals are based on. It only exists if your site gets enough traffic to be included — very low-traffic pages or brand-new sites may show "no data."
2. **Lab data** — a single simulated run (Lighthouse, PageSpeed Insights' lab panel, or a one-off scanner) under a fixed device/network profile. Useful for diagnosing *why* a page is slow and for testing changes before they ship, but it is not the number Google uses to judge you, and it will vary between runs because Chrome's simulated throttling isn't perfectly deterministic.

If your field data is bad but your lab score looks fine, that's usually a sign your real traffic skews toward older devices, slower connections, or geographies farther from your server/CDN edge than your own testing environment represents.

#### Common, fixable causes behind each metric

**LCP problems** are almost always one of: a slow server response (TTFB) before any bytes even reach the browser, a large hero image that isn't preloaded or isn't appropriately compressed/sized, or render-blocking CSS/JS delaying when the browser can even start painting. Preloading the actual LCP image (`<link rel="preload" as="image">`) and serving it at the display size (not a 4000px original scaled down in CSS) fixes a large share of LCP failures without touching anything else.

**INP problems** usually trace back to long JavaScript tasks blocking the main thread when a user interacts — think a click handler that triggers a big re-render, or third-party scripts (analytics, chat widgets, ad tech) hogging the thread at the worst moment. Breaking up long tasks (`scheduler.yield()`, or just chunking work manually) and auditing what your third-party tags are actually doing on interaction are the two highest-leverage fixes.

**CLS problems** come from content injecting itself after initial render without reserved space: images and embeds without explicit `width`/`height` (or `aspect-ratio` in CSS), ads that load into a container sized for nothing, and — very commonly — web fonts swapping in at a different x-height/width than the fallback font, shifting every line of text on the page. That last one is sneaky because it often doesn't show up in a quick desk-speed test; it only becomes visible on a throttled connection where the fallback font has time to render before the real one swaps in.

#### How to actually check where you stand

For field data, the most direct route is Google Search Console's Core Web Vitals report if you have the property verified, or the CrUX dashboard/API if you just want a quick read on a public URL. For a lab-based diagnostic — the thing you'll actually use while you're fixing something — PageSpeed Insights, Lighthouse in Chrome DevTools, and dedicated scanners will all give you per-metric breakdowns and a prioritized list of what's dragging each number down.
