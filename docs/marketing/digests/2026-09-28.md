**Drafted reply (answers both, no hard sell):**
> Good pushback — the study would land better with the "so what" attached. Quick version: CSP is the one that actually stops XSS payloads from executing even if you have an injection bug; HSTS closes the window where a user's first HTTP request could get intercepted before the redirect to HTTPS; X-Frame-Options/frame-ancestors stops clickjacking (your login button rendered invisibly inside someone else's iframe); X-Content-Type-Options: nosniff stops the browser from executing a file as script just because a MIME type got misconfigured. None of them fix a vulnerability — they reduce blast radius when something else goes wrong, which is why they're cheap and worth doing anyway.
>
> On the VPS question: no, hosts generally don't and shouldn't set these for you by default, because CSP in particular is app-specific — a policy that's safe for a static Astro site will break a site pulling in third-party widgets or inline scripts. Nginx/Caddy config snippets get you 90% of the way for static sites, but you still have to decide your own CSP directives. Mozilla's HTTP Observatory (observatory.mozilla.org) is a solid free scanner if you want a second opinion beyond the numbers in this post — I also run a free one at sitemechanic.io/security-analyzer.html that explains each missing header in plain language rather than just grading it, useful if you're handing the list to someone non-security to fix.

### What Is a Good Core Web Vitals Score in 2026?

If you've searched this, you've probably already found five different answers that don't quite agree. That's because "good Core Web Vitals score" gets used loosely — sometimes people mean the Lighthouse performance score (0–100), sometimes the three individual Core Web Vitals thresholds, and those are measured differently enough that a site can pass one and fail the other. Here's the actual breakdown, with the parts usually left out.

**The three metrics and their thresholds**

Core Web Vitals is three metrics, not one score:

- **LCP (Largest Contentful Paint)** — how long until the largest visible element (usually a hero image, a headline, or a banner) finishes rendering. Good: 2.5 seconds or less. Needs improvement: 2.5–4 seconds. Poor: over 4 seconds.
- **INP (Interaction to Next Paint)** — how long the page takes to visually respond after a user clicks, taps, or types, measured across every interaction in the session, not just the first one. Good: 200ms or less. Needs improvement: 200–500ms. Poor: over 500ms.
- **CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift)** — how much visible content jumps around unexpectedly while the page loads (that moment where you go to tap a button and an ad loads above it and you tap the wrong thing). Good: 0.1 or less. Needs improvement: 0.1–0.25. Poor: over 0.25.

INP replaced FID (First Input Delay) as the responsiveness metric back in March 2024 — if you're reading guidance that still talks about FID, it's stale. FID only measured the delay before the *first* interaction started processing; INP measures the full visual response time across *every* interaction in the session, which is a stricter and more honest number. Sites that scored well on FID sometimes score worse on INP, not because they got slower, but because INP is measuring something FID never caught.

**"Good" means the 75th percentile, not the average**

This is the part that trips people up. Google doesn't average your metric across visits — it looks at the 75th percentile. To pass, at least 75% of your real-world page loads need to hit the "good" threshold for each metric, at the URL level. A site that's blazing fast for 74% of visits and terrible for the rest doesn't pass, even though the average looks fine. This is deliberate: it's meant to catch the tail — the visitor on a throttled connection, an older phone, or a flaky network — not just the best-case demo load.

**Why your Lighthouse score and your Core Web Vitals status disagree**

This is the most common confusion, and it's worth being precise about because the two numbers measure genuinely different things:

- **Lighthouse** (and PageSpeed Insights' lab section) runs a single simulated page load, in a controlled environment, and gives you a 0–100 composite score. It's *lab data* — reproducible, but synthetic.
- **Core Web Vitals / CrUX** is aggregated from real Chrome users who actually visited your site over the last 28 days, on their real devices and real networks. It's *field data* — messier, but it's what Google actually uses for the "Core Web Vitals" status in Search Console and (to a limited degree) as a ranking signal.

A site can score 95 on Lighthouse and still fail Core Web Vitals in Search Console, usually because the lab run doesn't reflect your real traffic mix — if a meaningful share of your visitors are on mid-range Android phones on 4G, and your lab test runs on a fast simulated connection, you'll see the gap. The reverse also happens: a site with a mediocre Lighthouse score can pass CWV if the specific elements Lighthouse dings (unused JS, render-blocking resources) don't actually affect the three tracked metrics for most real visits.

If you only have time to check one thing, check CrUX/field data over lab data — it's what actually gates the "Core Web Vitals" label in Search Console, and unlike a single Lighthouse run, it isn't sensitive to whatever else your test machine was doing at the time.

**Does this actually affect SEO rankings?**

Yes, but modestly, and it's easy to overrate. Google has said page experience (which Core Web Vitals feeds into) is a ranking factor, but it's a tiebreaker-weight signal, not a dominant one — relevance and content quality still do most of the work. Where CWV matters more than the ranking algorithm suggests: conversion rate. Every major LCP study (Google's own included) shows abandonment climbing sharply past the 2.5–4 second mark, so even if Search treated it as a rounding error, your bounce rate wouldn't. Treat CWV as a user-experience metric that Google also happens to read, not an SEO hack.

**What actually moves each metric, in practice**

- **LCP** — almost always a hero image or a web font blocking render, occasionally a client-rendered hero section waiting on a JS bundle. Fixes, roughly in order of impact: `<link rel="preload">` the actual LCP image (not just `loading="eager"`), serve it in a modern format (AVIF/WebP) at the rendered size rather than scaled down by CSS, avoid rendering the hero content client-side if you can server-render or statically generate it, and preload the critical font file instead of waiting on a `@font-face` request to start after the CSSOM is built.
- **INP** — long JavaScript tasks blocking the main thread during an interaction. The usual suspects are heavy event handlers doing synchronous DOM work, layout thrashing (reading and writing layout properties in a loop), and third-party scripts — chat widgets, consent banners, analytics — running blocking work on click. Break up long tasks with `requestIdleCallback` or by yielding with `setTimeout(fn, 0)`/`scheduler.yield()`, and audit what your third-party scripts are actually doing on the interactions users complain about, not just on page load.
- **CLS** — almost always images or embeds without explicit `width`/`height` (or `aspect-ratio`) reserving their space before the asset loads, ads injected above existing content, or web fonts swapping in and reflowing text. For the font case specifically: use `font-display: swap` paired with a metric-matched fallback font (matched x-height and average character width) so the swap doesn't visibly reflow the layout — `font-display: swap` alone without a matched fallback often just moves the jank earlier rather than removing it.

**A quick gut-check before you go optimize**

Before spending a week on LCP, confirm it's actually your worst metric — teams often assume it's the image when the field data says it's INP from a third-party script they didn't write. Pull the CrUX breakdown per metric first, fix whichever one is failing hardest, and re-measure. Optimizing a metric that's already "good" for marginal gains is a worse use of time than fixing the one metric in the "poor" band, since Google's assessment is pass/fail per metric at the 75th percentile — there's no partial credit for a metric that goes from good to great.

**Checking your own numbers**

PageSpeed Insights (pagespeed.web.dev) is the standard free tool and pulls both lab and CrUX field data for any indexed URL — start there if you want Google's own numbers directly. If you want the same three metrics with plain-language flags for what to fix and where it's coming from, [check your Core Web Vitals for free with Site Mechanic](https://sitemechanic.io/core-web-vitals.html) — it's a ~30-second scan, no signup, and it's bundled with the rest of a full diagnostic (SEO, accessibility, security headers, fonts) rather than CWV in isolation, in case you want the fuller picture in one pass.
