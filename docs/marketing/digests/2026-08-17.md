---

Google's Core Web Vitals thresholds haven't moved much since INP replaced FID in
March 2024, but the number of developers who can recite them and still fail the
assessment has gone up. That gap is almost always the same root cause: confusing a
lab score (one Lighthouse run, on your machine, on fast wifi) with the field score
Google actually uses for ranking (real user data, 75th percentile, rolling 28 days).
This guide covers both — what "good" means, how the two measurements diverge, and
what's actually worth fixing first.

### The three metrics and their 2026 thresholds

**Largest Contentful Paint (LCP)** — time until the largest visible element
(usually a hero image, a headline, or a background image) finishes rendering.

- Good: ≤ 2.5s
- Needs improvement: 2.5s–4.0s
- Poor: > 4.0s

**Interaction to Next Paint (INP)** — measures the latency of *every* interaction
during a page's lifetime (clicks, taps, key presses) and reports the worst
representative one, not just the first. This is why INP is harder to fix than its
predecessor FID: a single laggy dropdown deep in a session can tank the whole score
even if the initial page load felt instant.

- Good: ≤ 200ms
- Needs improvement: 200ms–500ms
- Poor: > 500ms

**Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS)** — sum of all unexpected layout shifts during the
page's life, weighted by how much of the viewport moved and how far.

- Good: ≤ 0.1
- Needs improvement: 0.1–0.25
- Poor: > 0.25

A page needs "good" on all three, at the 75th percentile of visits, to get Google's
overall "Good" page-experience label. One metric in the "poor" bucket is enough to
sink the whole page, even if the other two are excellent — Google doesn't average.

### Why your Lighthouse score and your Search Console score disagree

This is the single most common source of confusion, and it's worth being blunt
about: **they're measuring different things, on purpose.**

- **Lab data** (Lighthouse, PageSpeed Insights' "diagnostics" section, most
  third-party checkers) runs a scripted, single-session test under simulated
  network/CPU throttling. It's reproducible and great for debugging, but it's one
  visit, from one location, on one device profile.
- **Field data** (the Chrome UX Report, "CrUX," which is what actually feeds
  Search Console's Core Web Vitals report and Google's ranking signal) aggregates
  real Chrome users who opted into telemetry, visiting your actual page, on their
  actual devices and networks, over a rolling 28-day window.

A site can score 95+ in Lighthouse and still show "Poor" LCP in Search Console,
because Lighthouse tested on a fast laptop and your real traffic is 60% mid-range
Android phones on 4G. The reverse also happens: a page that looks mediocre in
Lighthouse can pass in the field if your actual audience is mostly on fast
connections. If you only ever check the lab score, you're optimizing for a user who
might not represent your traffic at all.

Practical implication: use lab data (Lighthouse, or a checker like this one) to
*diagnose* — it tells you which specific request or element is slow and why. Use
field data (Search Console, CrUX) to *verify* — it tells you whether real visitors
are actually seeing the improvement. Shipping a fix and only checking Lighthouse
before declaring victory is the most common way teams end up "fixing" a metric that
doesn't move in Search Console for another month, because CrUX is a rolling
28-day average and needs that long to fully reflect a change.

### What's actually worth fixing, in order

1. **LCP first, almost always.** It's the easiest to move and the most visible.
   Common culprits: no `fetchpriority="high"` on the LCP image, the LCP element is
   lazy-loaded (don't lazy-load anything above the fold), render-blocking CSS/JS
   delaying first paint, or a slow server response (TTFB) pushing everything else
   back. Preloading the LCP resource and serving correctly-sized AVIF/WebP images
   typically gets more improvement per hour spent than anything else on this list.

2. **CLS second.** Almost always caused by images or ads without explicit
   `width`/`height` (or `aspect-ratio`), web fonts swapping in and reflowing text
   (see below), or content injected above existing content (cookie banners,
   dynamically loaded embeds). Reserve space for everything that loads after
   initial paint.

3. **INP last, but don't skip it.** It's the newest metric and the one teams are
   least practiced at diagnosing. Long JavaScript tasks on the main thread — a
   heavy event handler, a synchronous third-party script, an unoptimized React
   re-render — are the usual cause. Breaking up long tasks and deferring
   non-critical JS is the fix; there's rarely a single silver bullet the way
   `fetchpriority` is for LCP.

### One thing that's easy to miss: fonts affect two of the three metrics

Web fonts don't just look different — a `@font-face` swap that reflows text after
the page has already painted is a direct CLS hit, and a render-blocking font
request without `font-display: swap` or a preload hint can delay LCP if your
largest element is text. If you're auditing Core Web Vitals, check your font
loading strategy in the same pass; it's one of the few issues that shows up in
both metrics at once and is often overlooked because it doesn't feel like a
"performance" problem.

### Quick answers

**What is a good LCP score?** 2.5 seconds or faster, measured at the 75th
percentile of real visits (field data), not a single Lighthouse run.

**What is a good CLS score?** 0.1 or lower. Anything above 0.25 is flagged "poor"
and is almost always fixable by reserving space for images, embeds, and web fonts
before they load.

**What is a good INP score?** 200 milliseconds or faster. Unlike LCP and CLS, INP
is measured across the entire page session, not just the initial load — a page
that loads fast but freezes on a later click can still fail.

**Does Core Web Vitals affect Google rankings?** It's one of many ranking signals,
folded into Google's page experience assessment — not usually decisive on its own,
but a "Poor" rating can suppress otherwise-strong content, and it directly affects
whether a page is eligible for certain search features.
