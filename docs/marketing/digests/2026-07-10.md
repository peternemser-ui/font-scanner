### What Is a Good Core Web Vitals Score in 2026?

If you've stared at a Core Web Vitals report and wondered whether your numbers are "fine" or "a problem," you're not alone — the thresholds are simple, but the way Google actually applies them is not. Here's what "good" means in 2026, why your Lighthouse score and your Search Console report can disagree, and what to fix first.

#### The three metrics, and their thresholds

Core Web Vitals measure three things: how fast your main content appears, how responsive your page feels, and how much it jumps around while loading.

- **LCP (Largest Contentful Paint)** — time until the largest visible element (usually a hero image or headline) finishes rendering. **Good: ≤ 2.5s. Needs improvement: 2.5–4s. Poor: > 4s.**
- **INP (Interaction to Next Paint)** — the time between a user's click, tap, or keypress and the next visual update. This replaced FID (First Input Delay) in March 2024 and is still the interactivity metric in 2026. **Good: ≤ 200ms. Needs improvement: 200–500ms. Poor: > 500ms.**
- **CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift)** — how much visible content shifts unexpectedly during load. Measured as a unitless score. **Good: ≤ 0.1. Needs improvement: 0.1–0.25. Poor: > 0.25.**

To "pass" Core Web Vitals overall, you need all three in the "good" range — not an average across them. One poor metric fails the page even if the other two are excellent.

#### Why your score changes every time you check it

This is the part that trips people up: there are two entirely different measurement systems, and they rarely agree.

**Lab data** (what Lighthouse, PageSpeed Insights' "diagnostics" section, and most third-party checkers show you) is a single simulated page load on a specific device/network profile at a specific moment. Run it five times in a row and you'll get five slightly different numbers — that's normal, not a bug in the tool. Lab data is useful for debugging *why* something is slow, because it gives you a request waterfall and a breakdown of what blocked rendering.

**Field data** (what Google actually uses for ranking, sourced from the Chrome UX Report / CrUX) is real measurements from real Chrome users who visited your page over the last 28 days, rolled up at the **75th percentile**. That means a page only "passes" if at least 75% of real visits hit the "good" threshold — a handful of good runs on your own machine don't matter if a meaningful chunk of your actual visitors are on slow connections or old phones and experiencing something worse.

This is also why a perfect 100 Lighthouse score and a "Poor" Core Web Vitals report in Search Console can coexist — Lighthouse tested your machine's best case, CrUX reported everyone else's average case. If you only ever check lab data, you're grading yourself on easy mode.

#### Where problems actually come from, in order of frequency

1. **LCP, and it's almost always the hero image.** Oversized or unoptimized images (a 3MB PNG where a 100KB AVIF/WebP would do), missing `fetchpriority="high"` on the LCP element, and render-blocking CSS/JS ahead of it in the document are the three most common causes. Server response time (TTFB) is a distant fourth for most sites but worth ruling out if you're on shared or cold-starting hosting.
2. **CLS, and it's almost always missing dimensions.** Images and ads without explicit `width`/`height` (or `aspect-ratio`) reserve zero space until they load, then shove everything below them down the page. Web fonts that swap in with a different metrics profile than the fallback font cause the same effect — see below.
3. **INP, and it's almost always JavaScript doing too much on the main thread.** Long tasks blocking the thread when a user clicks something, third-party scripts (chat widgets, analytics, ad tech) competing for the same thread, and event handlers doing synchronous work that should be deferred or broken into smaller chunks.

#### The font connection nobody mentions

Since typography is easy to overlook here: web fonts are a legitimate CLS and LCP cause, not just a nice-to-have. If your custom font's fallback (system font) has different metrics — different average character width, different line height — the browser lays out text with the fallback, then reflows everything once the real font loads. That reflow is a layout shift, and if the font is used in your LCP element (a hero heading, for instance), it can also delay LCP directly. `font-display: swap` avoids invisible text but doesn't avoid the reflow; matching your fallback font's metrics with `size-adjust` (or picking a metrically-compatible fallback) actually fixes it.

#### The 30-minute self-check

You don't need five different dashboards to find your biggest problem:

1. Check **PageSpeed Insights** first — it's the only tool that shows you both lab data *and* your actual CrUX field data side by side, which tells you immediately whether you have a real problem or just lab noise.
2. If CrUX shows a real failing metric, use a lab tool with a request waterfall (Lighthouse, or a full-site scanner) to find the specific offending resource — which image, which script, which font.
3. Fix the single biggest offender first. LCP image compression alone resolves the majority of failing pages; don't start with INP micro-optimizations if your LCP is failing on a 3MB hero image.
4. Re-check field data in a week, not immediately — CrUX is a rolling 28-day window and won't reflect a fix you shipped an hour ago.
